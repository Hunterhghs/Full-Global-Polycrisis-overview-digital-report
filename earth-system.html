Overshoot

Ecological overshoot is the baseline underneath every other crisis

The concept from the 1972 Limits to Growth has stopped being a forecast and become a description: humanity now uses planetary resources faster than they regenerate and emits waste faster than the sinks absorb it. The measurable signature is the Earth's energy imbalance — about 1.36 watts per square metre of extra heat retained at the surface, per Hansen et al. — equivalent to nearly one million Hiroshima-scale bombs of surplus energy added to the system every single day.

That surplus is not stored quietly. It warms the ocean, which drives the storms, floods, heatwaves, and droughts that now fracture supply chains and displace populations. It also accelerates the warming rate itself: tropospheric temperature rose about 0.18°C per decade between 1970 and 2010, and is projected to rise 0.27°C per decade between 2010 and 2040 — a 50 percent acceleration. A faster rate is what makes tipping points closer than the headline temperature suggests, because the system has less time to absorb each increment.

Overshoot matters for the polycrisis because it converts every other stress into a moving target. A food system, a water system, or a health system built for the Holocene is now operating on a planet that has already left that equilibrium. The gap between what infrastructure assumes and what the earth system now delivers is the definition of unhedged risk — and the deficit is met not by markets but by the households least able to absorb it.

Nine boundaries, six breached

Two features of the table deserve emphasis. First, the breached boundaries are not independent failures; they are one metabolizing system. The nitrogen and phosphorus overload from agriculture drives coastal dead zones, which is the same chemistry as the deoxygenation boundary, which is the same physics as the ocean heat boundary. Second, the one recovering boundary — stratospheric ozone — recovered because a coordinated global protocol forced a substitute. It is the control experiment proving the polycrisis is a governance problem at least as much as a physics problem.

Biosphere

Biodiversity loss is the least reversible of the breaches

The IPBES Global Assessment found around one million animal and plant species threatened with extinction, many within decades — more than at any point in human history. Average native species abundance in most major land habitats has fallen by at least 20 percent, mostly since 1900. More than 40 percent of amphibian species, almost 33 percent of reef-forming corals, and more than a third of marine mammals are threatened.

The mechanism is not climate alone. Land-use change, overexploitation, pollution, and invasive species do most of the immediate damage; warming then amplifies it. IPBES found 22 of 44 assessed SDG-related targets undermined by negative nature trends. WWF's Living Planet Report 2024 put the decline in monitored wildlife populations at 73 percent between 1970 and 2020. The ecosystem services at stake — pollination, water purification, soil regeneration — are the load-bearing walls of the food system. Their decline converts biodiversity loss into food-price risk, and food-price risk into political risk.

The irreversibility is the analytical point. Carbon can be drawn back down, and a warming rate can be slowed, but an extinct species does not return when policy improves. Each lost population removes a node from the web of interactions that keep ecosystems functional — a web whose redundancy has been absorbing shocks for millennia. The biosphere boundary, once crossed far enough, does not bounce back; it re-organizes into a different, poorer state. That is why the Global Tipping Points Report treats biosphere tipping points — the Amazon, coral reefs — as the most consequential thresholds in the whole system.

Ocean

The ocean is losing oxygen and gaining acid at the same time

Ocean chemistry is changing on two fronts. Surface pH has dropped about 0.1 units since the pre-industrial era — roughly a 30 percent increase in acidity, per NOAA — with polar waters acidifying fastest. At the same time, the ocean has lost about 2 percent of its dissolved oxygen since the middle of the 20th century, and the volume of completely oxygen-depleted water has quadrupled since the 1960s, per IUCN.

The two stresses travel together: respiration consumes oxygen and releases carbon dioxide, so deoxygenation and acidification compound each other. Around 700 coastal sites were reporting low-oxygen conditions by 2011, up from 45 before the 1960s. Large predatory species — tuna, marlin, swordfish, sharks — are being squeezed into narrowing surface layers. Under a business-as-usual scenario, the ocean is projected to lose another 3–4 percent of its oxygen by 2100, with fisheries and the hundreds of millions of people who depend on them absorbing the cost. The eastern boundary upwelling systems alone support about one-fifth of the world's wild marine fish harvest.

The ocean is also the climate system's shock absorber — it has taken up roughly 90 percent of the excess heat from global warming, and about a quarter of anthropogenic CO₂. That buffering has a price, and the price is now being invoiced. The upper 2,000 metres added 16 zettajoules of heat between 2023 and 2024 alone, about 140 times the world's total electricity generation. A warmer, more stratified, more acidic ocean is a less productive ocean — and for the roughly 3 billion people who rely on marine protein, that is a food-security statement, not an oceanography footnote.

Land

Aridity is the under-reported half of the land story

The UNCCD's drying-lands assessment found 77.6 percent of Earth's land became permanently drier over the three decades to 2020 compared with the previous 30 years. Drylands expanded by 4.3 million square kilometres — an area larger than India — and now cover 40.6 percent of all land outside Antarctica. By 2100, up to five billion people could be living in drylands under a high-emissions trajectory.

Aridity is not drought. Drought ends; aridity is a permanent shift in the water balance that does not reverse when the rains return. It degrades soil, cuts crop yields, and drives migration from exactly the regions the exposure dossier identifies as the polycrisis epicentre: Sub-Saharan Africa, the Middle East, and South Asia. IPBES had already estimated that land degradation affects 3.2 billion people. The aridity data shows the trend is structural, not cyclical.

The economic channel is agriculture. Roughly 40 percent of the world's cropland sits in dryland regions, where yields fall sharply once the water balance tips. As aridity advances, the same land that produced grain for export produces grain for conflict. The UNCCD's framing is blunt: drying lands are a global threat because they undercut the food, water, and stability of the regions that can least afford the loss. Where the exposure dossier showed the human stack, the aridity data shows the physical stack underneath it.

Freshwater

One quarter of humanity lives where water runs out

WRI's Aqueduct Water Risk Atlas finds 25 countries — housing one quarter of the global population — face extremely high water stress, using at least 80 percent of their renewable supply every year. At least 50 percent of the world's population, around four billion people, live under highly water-stressed conditions for at least one month a year. In the Middle East and North Africa, 83 percent of the population is exposed to extreme stress; in South Asia, 74 percent.

The projection compounds the problem: by 2050, an additional one billion people are expected to live under extreme water stress even in an optimistic warming scenario, and water demand is projected to rise 20–25 percent. Thirty-one percent of global GDP — about $70 trillion — will be exposed to high water stress by 2050, up from $15 trillion in 2010. Water stress is where the earth-system boundary becomes a balance-sheet event, and where the energy system meets it: thermal power plants need cooling water, and a water-constrained grid is an electricity-constrained economy.

Water is also the system's most direct conflict vector. Sixty percent of the world's irrigated agriculture already faces extremely high water stress — sugarcane, wheat, rice, and maize above all — and those crops are the political crops. When a dam runs dry or a river basin is over-allocated, the dispute is not resolved by markets; it is resolved by power. The Cascade Institute counts water among the resources whose scarcity will drive the security crises of the coming decades, which is why the water table below reads like an early-warning board.

Cryosphere & Tipping Points

The cryosphere is where the thresholds are already visible

The Global Tipping Points Report 2025 states the new reality plainly: at 1.4°C of warming, warm-water coral reefs are crossing their thermal tipping point, and parts of the polar ice sheets may have crossed thresholds that eventually commit the world to several metres of sea-level rise. Below 2°C, the Amazon rainforest is at risk of widespread dieback and the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) is at risk of collapse.

These are not distant scenarios. Physics-based early-warning analysis published in Science Advances places the AMOC tipping point within 2025–2095 under the current trajectory. The Arctic has warmed nearly four times faster than the global average since 1979. Permafrost stores roughly twice the carbon currently in the atmosphere; as it thaws, it releases CO₂ and methane that accelerate the very warming causing the thaw. The State of the Cryosphere 2024 report concludes that current commitments, which lead toward well over 2°C, would bring irreversible ice loss with costs beyond adaptation limits for billions of people.

The cryosphere is the sharpest case of the report's central claim, because the feedbacks here are already measured, not modeled. The ice-albedo loop is visible in satellite data; the permafrost carbon release is visible in atmospheric monitoring; the slowing of polar ocean currents is now the growing scientific consensus noted by the State of the Cryosphere. These are the loops that, once closed, keep compounding even if emissions stop rising.

Tipping Cascades

Ecosystem tipping points do not stay inside their own ecosystem

Ecology's Regime Shifts Database maps thirty ecosystem critical transitions, and the comparative work of Rocha et al. shows they do not operate in isolation. Coral dieback removes the habitat that sustains reef fisheries, which shifts protein supply onto other systems, which stresses coastal economies. Amazon dieback cuts the moisture the forest itself generates, drying the basin, extending fire seasons, and weakening the rainfall that South American agriculture depends on. One system's regime shift becomes another system's stress.

This is the ecological version of the polycrisis's three pathways: common stresses weaken several ecosystems at once, domino effects pass a collapse from one ecosystem to the next, and inter-systemic feedbacks lock two collapses together. The climate–biodiversity nexus is the clearest case — warming stresses species, species loss degrades the carbon sinks, weaker sinks accelerate the warming. The loop is already measurable.

Warming → Species stress → Weaker carbon sinks → Faster warming the climate–biodiversity nexus

The cascade concept changes what counts as an early warning. Monitoring a single ecosystem's health misses the point when the danger is the coupling between ecosystems. A reef, a forest, and a permafrost region are separate places with separate science; in the polycrisis they are one network, and the signal to watch is whether a shift in one is transmitting to the others.

Pollution

Novel entities: the ninth boundary has already been crossed

The planetary-boundaries framework treats "novel entities" — plastics, synthetic chemicals, and other human-made materials — as its own boundary, and the 2023 update found it already breached. The world produces roughly 400 million tonnes of plastic waste a year, about 11 million tonnes of it entering the ocean annually, on track to triple by 2040. Plastic pollution has increased tenfold since 1980, per IPBES.

Chemical and nutrient pollution run on the same compounding logic. Excess nitrogen and phosphorus from agriculture drive the coastal eutrophication that accelerates deoxygenation. The pollution boundary and the ocean boundary are not separate problems; they are two ends of the same pipe.

The health channel is the least visible and the most personal. Microplastics have been found in human blood, placenta, and lung tissue; per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) contaminate drinking water on every inhabited continent; and the chemical load of modern life is now a plausible contributor to the chronic disease burden that aging health systems are least equipped to carry. Pollution, like AMR on the Systemic Domains page, is a slow pandemic — its mortality is diffuse, its latency is long, and its causes are the same production systems driving the other boundaries.

The Transition Trap

The disorderly-transition catch-22

The window for an orderly transition to net zero has arguably closed. Delayed policy action now means that aggressive, abrupt carbon reduction will eventually be required — disrupting carbon-intensive industries, stranding trillions of dollars in fossil assets, and displacing workers concentrated in the old energy economy. Failing to act guarantees escalating physical climate risk instead. Leaders face a paralyzing choice: accept severe near-term economic pain, or accept compounding long-term existential pain.

In a polarized, short-term election cycle, the political calculus usually favors delay — which deepens the polycrisis and raises the eventual cost of both options. This is not an argument against transition. It is the reason the transition itself has become one of the system's stresses: the longer it is postponed, the more disruptive its arrival will be, and the more the disruption will be blamed on the transition rather than on the delay.

The positive-tipping-points literature is the escape hatch, and it matters that it is real. Solar and battery costs have fallen along curves no mainstream forecast predicted; electric-vehicle adoption has tipped in major markets; climate litigation is spreading contagiously. The Global Tipping Points Report 2025 concludes that a minority of actors can still tip the majority. The catch-22, in other words, is genuine but not final — it is a political trap, not a physical one.
